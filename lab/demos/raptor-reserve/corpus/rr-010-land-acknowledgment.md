# Land Acknowledgment

Category: Governance
Date: 2026-06-01
Author: Helen Harrow
Source: Raptor Reserve internal record

Raptor Reserve sits on the traditional territory of the Wabanaki peoples, including the Passamaquoddy, Penobscot, Maliseet, and Mi'kmaq. These nations have lived on and cared for this coast for thousands of years. They fished the waters, gathered along the shore, and moved with the seasons long before the island had a lighthouse or a reserve.

The reserve does not own this history, and an acknowledgment alone does not repair it. The board commits to three actions: invite Wabanaki representatives to the annual planning meeting, support tribal youth internships in conservation, and review the reserve's archaeological policy with tribal historic preservation officers.

The island was transferred to the trust in 1987. The reserve recognizes that the transfer is part of a longer history of dispossession and that the record is incomplete.

The reserve asks visitors to treat the island as a living place, not a museum. Do not remove stones, feathers, or plants. Do not enter closed areas. If you meet a tribal representative or a researcher, listen first. The acknowledgment is a starting point, not a substitute for relationship.

This statement was approved by the board on 1 June 2026. It is reviewed every three years with tribal partners.

## Scope

Land Acknowledgment applies to the board, the director, full-time and seasonal staff, volunteers, researchers, and contractors working on Blacktail Island. It does not replace the charter (rr-001), the emergency response plan (rr-006), or the safety plan (rr-005). When two documents disagree, the charter wins, then the management plan, then the specific policy.

## Roles and responsibilities

The board approves the document. The director, Dr. Mara Voss, owns implementation and reports exceptions to the board. The head ranger, Elin Ward, is the day-to-day contact for field staff. The data manager, Samir Haddad, keeps the signed copy in the document register and makes sure the shared drive has the current version.

## What staff should do

Read the document during onboarding. Ask the director or the head ranger if a situation is not covered. Record decisions that deviate from the document, including the reason and the date. Do not edit the shared copy. Send proposed changes to the data manager.

## Records and evidence

The reserve keeps the approved version, the board minutes, the public comment summary, and the annual review memo. Records are kept for 10 years. Personnel details are stored separately and are not part of the public corpus.

## Review and amendment

Review follows the schedule in the document. If the document does not set one, the default is every two years. Proposed changes go to the board packet 14 days before the meeting. A charter change needs a two-thirds vote; other documents need a simple majority.

## Common failure modes

The most common problems are outdated copies, unclear ownership, and field decisions that are never written down. The reserve fixes these by naming one owner, using a single document register, and reviewing the field log at the Monday safety meeting.
