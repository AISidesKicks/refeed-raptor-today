# Northern harrier

Category: Species and ecology
Date: 2026-03-01
Author: Tomas Reed
Source: Raptor Reserve internal record

Northern harrier (Circus hudsonius) is a state special concern raptor in the reserve's monitoring program. In 2025, the reserve monitored 6 breeding pairs, which fledged 8 chicks. The birds nest in Heron Marsh and the South Beach grassland. Their diet is mainly meadow voles and small birds.

The 2025 nest checks used the standard three-visit protocol: occupancy in April, stage and clutch size in May, and chick count in June. Observers stayed at least 150 m from active nests. Harriers are the reserve's main vole predator.

The main threats are marsh drainage, nest trampling by deer, and low vole years. The reserve manages them with seasonal trail closures, a ban on lead ammunition, and a 250 m no-fly zone around active nests.

Visitors should watch from the marked viewpoints and keep 100 m from any bird that is alarm-calling or carrying food. If a bird leaves a nest because of a visitor, the visitor should back away slowly and report the encounter to a ranger.

## Field identification

Northern harrier is a slender hawk with a white rump patch and a low, buoyant flight. It is most often confused with the rough-legged hawk, which is larger and has a different wing pattern. The scientific name is Circus hudsonius. At the reserve, the best clues are the flight silhouette, the wing pattern, and the habitat.

## Habitat and territory

The species nests in Heron Marsh and the South Beach grassland. The reserve monitors 6 territories. Observers check the territories from the ground and stay at least 150 m from active nests. The birds use the same territory across years when the site is undisturbed.

## Diet and hunting

The diet is mainly meadow voles and small birds. The birds hunt by coursing low over the marsh and pouncing on prey. Pellets and prey remains are collected for the diet study (rr-047) when they are found away from nests.

## Breeding timeline

Courtship begins in April. Eggs are laid in May, hatch in June, and the chicks fledge in July. The reserve's three-visit protocol matches this schedule: occupancy in April, stage and clutch size in May, and chick count in June.

## Monitoring results

The 2025 season recorded 6 pairs and 8 fledged chicks. The 2024 season recorded 6 pairs and 7 fledged chicks. The long-term trend is reported in the annual breeding summary (rr-043) and the occupancy analysis (rr-045).

## Threats and management

The lead paragraph lists the main threats. The management response has three parts: seasonal trail closures, a ban on lead ammunition, and a 250 m no-fly zone around active nests. Nest platforms are added where a natural site is unstable. The management plan target is to protect at least 90 percent of occupied territories.

## Visitor guidance

The lead paragraph gives the distance rule. The short version: stay on the marked trail, keep 100 m from a bird that is alarm-calling or carrying food, and do not use playback, drones, or food to get a closer look. Report injured birds to a ranger.
