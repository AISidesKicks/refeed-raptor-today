# Osprey

Category: Species and ecology
Date: 2026-03-01
Author: Tomas Reed
Source: Raptor Reserve internal record

Osprey (Pandion haliaetus) is a state special concern raptor in the reserve's monitoring program. In 2025, the reserve monitored 14 breeding pairs, which fledged 21 chicks. The birds nest in platform nests and dead snags around Seal Cove and Heron Marsh. Their diet is mainly fish, mostly menhaden and mackerel.

The 2025 nest checks used the standard three-visit protocol: occupancy in April, stage and clutch size in May, and chick count in June. Observers stayed at least 150 m from active nests. The oldest known nest platform was built in 1998.

The main threats are nest platform damage, fishing line entanglement, and low fish availability in cold springs. The reserve manages them with seasonal trail closures, a ban on lead ammunition, and a 250 m no-fly zone around active nests.

Visitors should watch from the marked viewpoints and keep 100 m from any bird that is alarm-calling or carrying food. If a bird leaves a nest because of a visitor, the visitor should back away slowly and report the encounter to a ranger.

## Field identification

Osprey is a large fish hawk with a white head, a dark eye stripe, and angled wings. It is most often confused with the bald eagle, which has a white head and tail but a different flight profile. The scientific name is Pandion haliaetus. At the reserve, the best clues are the flight silhouette, the wing pattern, and the habitat.

## Habitat and territory

The species nests in platform nests and dead snags around Seal Cove and Heron Marsh. The reserve monitors 14 territories. Observers check the territories from the ground and stay at least 150 m from active nests. The birds use the same territory across years when the site is undisturbed.

## Diet and hunting

The diet is mainly fish, mostly menhaden and mackerel. The birds hunt by hovering over the water and plunging feet-first. Pellets and prey remains are collected for the diet study (rr-047) when they are found away from nests.

## Breeding timeline

Pairs return in late March. Eggs are laid in late April, hatch in late May, and the chicks fledge in July. The reserve's three-visit protocol matches this schedule: occupancy in April, stage and clutch size in May, and chick count in June.

## Monitoring results

The 2025 season recorded 14 pairs and 21 fledged chicks. The 2024 season recorded 14 pairs and 19 fledged chicks. The long-term trend is reported in the annual breeding summary (rr-043) and the occupancy analysis (rr-045).

## Threats and management

The lead paragraph lists the main threats. The management response has three parts: seasonal trail closures, a ban on lead ammunition, and a 250 m no-fly zone around active nests. Nest platforms are added where a natural site is unstable. The management plan target is to protect at least 90 percent of occupied territories.

## Visitor guidance

The lead paragraph gives the distance rule. The short version: stay on the marked trail, keep 100 m from a bird that is alarm-calling or carrying food, and do not use playback, drones, or food to get a closer look. Report injured birds to a ranger.
